\subsection{Motivation}

Drone based services are becoming increasingly popular in various industries such as agriculture and delivery services.
The adoption of drone services has been limited by public perception and legislation.
A key factor influencing public perception is the noise generated by drones, which is often perceived as intrusive and annoying.
Propulsive efficiency is also important for feasibility as it directly affects flight time and payload capacity.

Recent work has explored innovative methods for reducing noise without compromising performance.
One such propeller design, commonly referred to as toroidal, features looped blades and is known in marine applications.
These have been shown experimentally to reduce Overall Sound Pressure Level (OASPL) and Psychoacoustic Annoyance (PA) factors \cite{shima_toroidal}, \cite{ijerph20031955}.
Such PA factors indicate noise at discrete frequencies, or tones, to be a major source of annoyance in propeller noise \cite{ijerph20031955}. %using an extension of Zwicker's psychoacoustic model \cite{DI2016164}.

Mechanisms for tonal noise reduction of standard propellers through sweep and uneven blade spacing are well understood \cite{hanson_influence}, \cite{DOBRZYNSKI1993123}, \cite{doi:10.2514/3.22938}.
However this acoustic theory has not been applied to looped designs, which feature unevenly spaced and highly swept blades.
Looped propellers structure also allows for larger sweep than standard propellers \cite{shima_toroidal}
A fast method to predict the tonal noise would enable iterative design methods to optimise the looped propeller design for noise reduction.

\section{Theory}
% sentence about the theory of this report not going into a lot of detail because
% of the broad scope of the report but will be crucial nevertheless

This section outlines theory behind aerodynamic and acoustic performance of propellers.
Due to the broad scope of this report, only the most relevant principles will be briefly introduced.
Despite this brevity, these concepts are central to the objective of reducing noise for a given efficiency, or increasing efficiency for a given noise level.
The theory will show how noise and efficiency are inherently coupled, providing a foundation for comparing different propeller designs.

\subsection{Acoustic definitions and relationships}
% acoustic side
Sound is a pressure fluctuation in a medium.
The overall sound pressure level (OASPL) is defined as the root mean square of the sound pressure, normalised to a reference pressure.
Its expressed logarithmically, as shown in equation \ref{eq:OASPL}.
Noise at discrete frequency intervals will later be shown to be of importance and so a definition of this frequency dependent sound pressure level (SPL) is provided in equation \ref{eq:SPL}.

\begin{equation}
    OASPL = 20 \log_{10} \left( \frac{p_\text{rms}}{p_\text{ref}} \right) \label{eq:OASPL}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    SPL(m) = 10 \log_{10} \frac{\int^{m f_0 + \varepsilon}_{m f_0 - \varepsilon} PSD(f) df }{p^2_\text{ref}} \label{eq:SPL}
\end{equation}

% introduce intuitively how the sound pressure level would be expected to vary with speed, diameter, distance
Intuitively the SPL of propeller noise would vary with the pressure difference accross the blade.
It is then expected that the SPL would vary with tip speed squared, where the pressure differential is greatest.
The pressure differential will also depend on local aerodynamic coefficients but this effect is secondary.
From conservation of energy the sound intensity must be an inverse square law with distance.
The sound energy can be shown to be proportional to the square of the sound pressure level...
This means that the sound pressure level will vary with distance in an inverse law.
Equation \ref{eq:expected_plaw} shows the general form of the expected sound pressure level from a propeller.

\begin{equation}
    p_\text{rms} = \frac{\rho (r \Omega)^2}{R/r} g(\theta, \text{geometry}) \label{eq:expected_plaw}
\end{equation}

Where $g()$ is a non-dimensional function which accounts for directivity of the observer and geometry of the propeller.
The dependence on $\theta$ is due to interference patterns as the propeller is similar in size to the wavelengths of sound that it emits.
This means sound generated from different pressure differentials along the blade span, or chord, will interfere.
To build a picture of the function $g()$ its required to model the sound generated over the entire surface of the propeller.

\subsection{Noise from arbitrary rotating surfaces}
% how do we model our designs?
% generalised function of fluid domain, define surface from source distribution
% explain why these are modelled as monopole, dipole and quadrupole sources respectively
Ffowcs Williams, following from the work of Lighthill \cite{lighthill_1952}, rearranged the Navier stokes equations to account for sound generation by surfaces in motion \cite{ffowcs_1969}.
He demonstrated that noise arises from three mechanisms: unsteady displacement, unsteady loading and turbulence.
For the benefit of understanding, an intuitive explaination is given here.
Consider a general flow field with a surface in unsteady motion.
To enforce the 0 surface normal velocity boundary condition on the surrounding fluid, a mass flow source is required.
This is a monopole source, which is a point source of sound that radiates equally in all directions.
To balance the rate of momentum imparted to the surface, a momentum source is required in the direction of the force.
This dipole source is equivilent to a pair of monopole mass flow sources, with opposing strengths, infitisesimally close together.
A third type of source, called a quadrupole, can originate from turbulence within the flow field and interact with the surface.
Calculation of the quadrupole source is more involved and outside the scope of this report.
%To enforce shear and normal stress originating from turbulence, a quadrupole source is required.
%This forms a tensor with one dimension being direction of force and the other being the direction of fluctuation.


% can now see why a rotating propeller requires sources to be convected helicoidally
% by neglecting turbulent noise, show harmonic noise is the only remaining source
% in absence of BVI
Now consider a single blade in hover with sources distributed on its surface.
As the blade rotates with angular speed $\Omega$, the sources are convected through a helical path.
This is from the circular motion of the blade, and a small induced axial velocity (see section \ref{sec:actuator_disk}).
From a stationary observer's perspective, the source strength, distance, and relative velocity all vary periodically.
There is then also spatial periodicity from $B$ evenly spaced blades.


Hanson \cite{hanson_theory} considered the distribution of sources convected along a helicoidal surface.
His analysis in the frequency domain reduced the problem to frequencies at integers, or harmonics, of the blade passage frequency.
This showed cancellation of noise at all other frequencies.

% he distributed sources on the chord
% 

% explain why the integral of many helically convected sources models the propeller
% explain how changing location of sources can cause destructive interference for some wavenumbers

If the noise consists of only harmonics of the blade passage frequency for $B$ blades, then it can be written as an inverse fourier transform.
\begin{equation}
    p'(t) = \sum_{m=-\infty}^\infty P_{mB} \exp(-jmB\Omega t)
\end{equation}
The noise can then expressed as the sum of thickness, drag and lift terms with the respective subscripts, $P_{mB} = P_{Vm} + P_{Dm} + P_{Lm}$.

    \begin{aligned}
        \begin{bmatrix}
            P_{Vm} \\

            P_{Lm}
        \end{bmatrix} = - \frac{\rho_0 (r_t \Omega)^2}{4\pi (r / r_t)} \exp\left(jmB \left( \frac{\Omega r}{c_0} - \frac{\pi}{2} \right)\right) \\
        \times \int_0^1 z^2 \exp(j(\phi_0 + \phi_s)) J_{mB} \left( \frac{mB z \Omega r_t}{c_0} \sin \theta \right) \begin{bmatrix}
            k_x^2 t_b \Psi_V(k_x) \\
            jk_x (C_D / 2) \Psi_D(k_x) \\
            -jk_y (C_L / 2) \Psi_L(k_x)
        \end{bmatrix}dz
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:hansons_result}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Aerodynamic definitions and relationships}

% explain the origin of thrust and torque
The thrust of a propeller is the axial force exerted by the air on the propeller due to imparted momentum and profile losses.
The torque is the rotational force exerted by the air on the propeller and is a result of imparted angular momentum to the air and profile losses.
% actuator theory
% dependence of these aeroydynamic forces with speed, diameter, introduce invarient coefficients
% thrust and torque coefficients

% torque, thrust coefficients and FoM definition

Torque and thrust coefficients are defined as
\begin{equation}
    C_T = \frac{T}{\frac{1}{2} \rho (r\omega)^2 A} \quad \quad \quad C_Q = \frac{Q}{\frac{1}{2} \rho (r\omega)^2 A r} \label{eq:CT_CQ}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    FM = \frac{C_T^{3/2}}{\sqrt{2}C_Q} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \rho}} \frac{T^{3/2}}{Q} \frac{1}{R}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Actuator Disk Theory \label{sec:actuator_disk}}

The propeller can be modelled is a thin disk with a uniform pressure distribution.
Incompressible, inviscid flow is assumed, with zero exit swirl velocity so $p_3 = p_0$.
Conservation of momentum and energy lead to the following equations.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{subfigure}[t]{0.4\textwidth}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{../tikz/actuator_disk.pdf}
        \caption{Actuator disk.}
        \label{fig:actuator_disk}
    \end{subfigure}
    \begin{subfigure}[t]{0.5\textwidth}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{../tikz/streamtube.pdf}
        \caption{Annular streamtube.}
        \label{fig:actuator_annulus}
    \end{subfigure}
    \caption{}
\end{figure}
\begin{align}
    T &= \dot{m} w = \rho A v w \label{eq:actuator_disk_momentum} \\ 
    Tv &= \frac{1}{2} \dot{m} w^2 \label{eq:actuator_disk_energy}
\end{align}
Equation \ref{eq:actuator_disk_energy} can also be obtained through Bernoulli's equation by setting $p_2 - p_1 = T/A$.
Substituting the momentum equation into energy, the key result $w = 2v$ is obtained.
This gives the thrust force as only a function of induced velocity.
\begin{equation}
    T = 2 \rho v^2 A \label{eq:actuator_disk_thrust}
\end{equation}
Remember for the acoustic model, aerodynamic forces are required to be known along the entire surface of the propeller.
The above analysis is not limited to the entire disk, and can be applied to an annulus element.
\begin{equation}
    \delta T = 2 \rho v^2 \delta A
\end{equation}

However, the local induced velocity $v$ is not known and more information is required to solve for the local thrust.
This comes from blade element theory discussed in the next section.

\subsection{Blade Element Theory}
% its more complicated than that, BET: nonlinear version of airfoil theory
% justify why its more complicated than that. we dont want to overcomplicate things
Blade element theory (BET) divides a blade into annular airfoil elements.
Each section is assumed to be a 2D airfoil with local lift and drag coefficients.
These are dependent on the local angle of attack and Reynolds number which can be calculated from induced and operating velocities.
The local aerodynamic forces are then calculated and then resolved in axial and tangential directions for thrust and torque respectively.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{../tikz/bem.pdf}
    \caption{Aerodynamic forces on local blade element}
    \label{fig:airfoil}
\end{figure}
\begin{equation}
    \alpha = \beta - \phi = \beta - \tan^{-1} \left( \frac{v}{\Omega r} \right) \label{eq:angle_of_attack}
\end{equation}
Where the resolved aerodyanmic forces are
\begin{align}
    \delta T = \frac{1}{2} \rho W^2 ( c_l \cos(\phi) - c_d \sin(\phi) ) \label{eq:normal_force} \\
    \delta Q = \frac{1}{2} \rho W^2 r ( c_l \sin(\phi) + c_d \cos(\phi) ) \label{eq:tangential_force}
\end{align}
With two equations for local thrust from the induced velocity, a solution must be found iteratively.
This is due to the unavoidable nonlinearity of the problem that arises from the varying rotational speed along the radius.
This is done numerically until the change in inducded velocity becomes sufficiently small.

% explain that this doesnt include swirl or tip losses where the blade element theory breaks down
Its important to note that a number of simplifications were made in this implementation of BET.
Firstly, swirl in the wake is neglected, which typically increases induced velocity and induced power by 2\% \cite{?}.
Conservation of angular momentum alongside discussed momentum theory can solve for an induced swirl velocity $u$ used to calculate BET variables.
\begin{equation}
    \phi = \tan \left( \frac{v}{\Omega r + u} \right)
\end{equation}
Tip losses were also neglected, where blade element theory assumptions break down.
Prandtl derived a tip loss factor for the additional induced velocity near the tip, based on a simple horseshoe wake model \cite{prandtl_loss}. 
% more advanced wake models exist.

The airfoil coefficients were assumed to be constant over the range of Reynolds numbers considered, only varying with angle of attack.

\subsection{Experimental}
% where was the testing done
The experimental work was carried out in the acoustics lab at University of Cambridge's Department of Engineering.
% justification for separating acoustic and aerodynamic measurements
Two separate setups were used to measure the aerodynamic and acoustic performance of the propellers.
The primary reason for this separation was to ensure better measurements in each domain.
The setups were designed to have near identical propeller and motor conditions, allowing for a direct comparison of the results.
The drone motor, Emax ECO II Series 2207 1700KV, was used to drive the propellers with an Odrive S1 motor controller.
This controller allowed for precise measurement and control of both motor speed and torque.
A high speed AS5047P encoder was used to measure the angular speed of the motor.
The same 6 cell, 3300 mAh lithium polymer battery was also used to power the motor for both setups.


\subsection{Saftey}
The safety of the experimental setup was a key consideration throughout the project.
The testing required high speed rotating propellers which could cause serious injury.
The custom 3D printed propellers were expected to be pushed to their material limits and so the risk of failure was high.
For these reasons, all 3D printed propellers were tested soley in the foam anechoic chamber with the door closed and the operator outside.

\subsection{Ffowcs Williams Equation}

For a function $f(\mathbf{x}, t)$ that defines a surface $ S(t) = \{\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3 : f(\mathbf{x}, t) = 0\}$ the density purturbation $\rho$ from the motion of the surface satisfies the wave equation:
\begin{equation}
    c_0^2 \square \rho = \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_i \partial x_j} T_{ij} + \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \rho_0 v_n |\nabla f| \delta(f) \right) - \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} \left( p_{ij} n_j |\nabla f| \delta(f) \right)
\end{equation}
This result is a direct re-arrangement of the Navier-Stokes equations, where $T_{ij}$ is the stress tensor, $p_{ij}$ is the pressure tensor, and $n_j$ is the normal vector to the surface.
