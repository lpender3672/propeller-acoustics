\subsection{Motivation}

Drone based services are becoming increasingly popular in various industries such as agriculture and delivery services \cite{?}.
However the adoption of drone services has been limited by public perception and legislation \cite{?}.
A key factor influencing public perception is the noise generated by drones, which is often perceived as intrusive and annoying \cite{?}.
Propulsive efficiency is also important for feasibility as it directly affects flight time and payload capacity \cite{?}.
This project aims to investigate acoustic sources of noise in quadcopter propellers and reduce the noise generated by these propellers.

Recent literature has focused on a looped propeller design for eVTOL, known in marine applications.
Experimental work has verified a reduction in OASPL and in psychoacoustic annoyance factors \cite{shima_toroidal}, \cite{ijerph20031955}.
Tonal noise is a major source of annoyance in propeller noise \cite{ijerph20031955} which has been shown experimentally to be reduced in the looped propeller design.
The mechanisms of harmonic noise reduction of the looped propeller design are thought to be due to sweep and uneven blade spacing \cite{?}.
Such mechanisms are well understood for standard propellers in forward flight \cite{hanson_influence}, \cite{DOBRZYNSKI1993123}, \cite{doi:10.2514/3.22938}.
However a fast low fidelity method implementing this theory to predict noise from looped propellers is missing.
Such a method would enable iterative design methods to optimise the looped propeller design for noise reduction.

Looped propellers improved structure allows for larger sweep and hence can achieve greater noise reduction than standard propellers \cite{shima_toroidal} ???.

Hypothesis:
The harmonic noise of loop propellers can be modelled by the superposition of evenly spaced, swept propellers.

\subsection{BEM}

Blade Element Momentum theory is a common method to predict the aerodynamic performance of propellers.
BEM is a simplification of lifting line theory in that the induced velocity at each annular section is assumed to be due to only the forces on that section.
Then actuator disk theory is combined with momentum theory to create a system of non-linear equations which can be solved iteratively.

\subsection{Hansons Helicoidal Surface Theory}
Theory developed by Hanson predicts the far field harmonic noise from propellers.

\begin{equation}
    p(t) = \sum_{m=-\infty}^\infty P_{mB} \exp(-jmB\Omega t)
\end{equation}

The noise harmonic is decomposed into thickness and loading as per the Lighthill analogy.

\begin{equation}
    P_{mB} = P_{Vm} + P_{Dm} + P_{Lm}
\end{equation}

Then Hanson derived the far field noise from Goldsteins version of the acoustic analogy.
Thin airfoil theory is used, along with generalised forms of thickness and loading, to derive a generalised source function.
A chordwise spatial fourier transform of source function then separates the contributions of different wavenumbers (spatial frequencies) along the chord.
The resulting chordwise wavenumber is shown to depend on the time harmonic showing periodicity in both space and time which is expected from intuition.

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \begin{bmatrix}
            P_{Vm} \\
            P_{Dm} \\
            P_{Lm}
        \end{bmatrix} = - \frac{\rho_0 c_0^2 \sin(\theta )\exp\left(jn \left( \frac{\Omega r}{c_0} - \frac{\pi}{2} \right)\right)}{4\pi r} \\
        \times \int M_r^2 \exp(j(\phi_0 + \phi_s)) J_{mB} \left( \frac{mB\Omega r_0}{c_0} \sin \theta \right) \begin{bmatrix}
            k_x^2 t_b \Psi_V(k_x) \\
            jk_x (C_D / 2) \Psi_D(k_x) \\
            -jk_y (C_L / 2) \Psi_L(k_x)
        \end{bmatrix}dz
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Where $\Psi$ is the chordwise spatial fourier transform of the respective sectional thickness, drag and lift distributions.
The distributions are normalised such that $\Psi(0) = 1$.
\begin{equation}
    \Psi_V(k_x) = \int_{-1/2}^{1/2} H(X) \exp(jk_x X) dX
\end{equation}
With wavenumbers and sectional phase lags given by
\begin{equation}
    k_x = \frac{mB b}{r_0}, \quad
    k_y = \frac{mB\Omega b}{z c_0} \cos \theta, \quad
    \phi_0 = k_y \frac{FA}{b}, \quad
    \phi_s = k_x \frac{MCA}{b}, \quad
\end{equation}


The spanwise integral is performed over a number of discrete airfoil sections.

Directivity

$\Psi(k_x)$ is the chordwise spatial fourier transform which accounts for \textit{non-compactness} of the distributed sources.
The far field noise from non-compact sources is a result of interference and phase lag. 
This explains its dependence on wavenumber and normalised source strengths.

Hanson also showed that strong peaks in the chordwise lift distribution should be avoided
as these lead to compact, high amplitude noise sources that are difficult to cancel out.
A near uniform loading is shown to have destructive interference at some wavenumbers \cite{hanson_influence}.
However, strong suction peaks are a standard feature of high lift to drag ratio airfoils \cite{?}
and so are difficult to avoid without compromising significantly on aerodynamic performance.


For a given harmonic, the spanwise integral can be written as a sum of complex vectors representing the far field noise from each section.
The destructive interference of each component can then easily be visualised in head-to-tail vector addition seen below.

The problem for minimising noise of a given harmonic is then to sweep the blade in such a way that minimises the final magnitude of the vector summation.
The magnitudes of the vectors will be assumed fixed ie constant thickness and loading.

\subsection{Experimental setup}

To validate the theoretical framework, designed propellers will be manufactured and tested both aerodynamically and acoustically.
The acoustic test will be conducted in an anechoic chamber with a small microphone array to capture directivity of harmonic components.
An attempt to isolate the propeller noise from the motor will be made by placing the motor in a foam box.
Background and motor noise will be measured before and after each test series to ensure repeatability.??
Mechanical motor is primarily due to structural vibrations from forced unsteady radial loads \cite{motor_vibration}.

For the aerodynamic test, the thrust and torque of the propeller will be measured on a setup.
Strain gauge load cells were chosen for force measurement over null methods for simplicity and the potential to measure dynamic loads.
The propeller will be mounted upside down to prevent the ground effect, and such that the force is in the direction and range of calibration loads.
It will also be at a 45 degree angle such that a graviational mass can calibrate the torque load cell.

Large currents in the motor can induce voltages in the strain gauge wire which can interfere with the measurement.
Efforts to mitigate this include twisted pair wiring and shielding the wires.
