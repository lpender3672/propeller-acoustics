\subsection{Thrust and Torque}

There are two widely used methods to measure forces.
The first is a null method which involves adjusting a mechanical balance to zero the force.
The second, deflection method, measures force by measuring the strain.

\begin{table}[h!]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{|l|l|l|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Method} & \textbf{Advantages} & \textbf{Disadvantages} \\
        \hline
        Null Method & High accuracy, No calibration, No drift & Complicated setup, Cannot measure dynamic forces \\
        \hline
        Deflection Method & Simple setup, Can measure dynamic forces & Calibration required, Drift \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}

\end{table}

It is decided to use strain gauges to measure dynamic forces as the setup is simpler and the forces are expected to be dynamic.
The strain gauge arrangement is important as to eliminate forces in directions other than the required thrust and torque.

The ranges of forces are important to consider when choosing the arrangement.
From preliminary tests, the ranges of thrust and torque are expected to be 0.1-10N and 0.001-0.1Nm respectively.

Some theory of stain gauges is required to position them correctly.
The resistance of a wire is given by
\begin{equation}
    R = \frac{\rho L}{A} \label{eq:resistance}
\end{equation}
A small change in resistance is simply
\begin{equation}
    \delta R = \frac{\partial R}{\partial L} \delta L + \frac{\partial R}{\partial A} \delta A + \frac{\partial R}{\partial \rho} \delta \rho
\end{equation}
Substituting in equation \ref{eq:resistance} and factoring out the strain, $\epsilon$ with Poisson's ratio $\nu$ gives
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\delta R}{R} = \frac{\delta L}{L} + \frac{\delta A}{A} + \frac{\delta \rho}{\rho} = (1 + 2\nu)\epsilon + \frac{\delta \rho}{\rho}
\end{equation}
A fundamental parameter of strain gauges is its sensitivity to strain, which is labelled the gauge factor.
\begin{equation}
    G_F = \frac{\Delta R/R}{\epsilon} = (1 + 2\nu) + \frac{1}{\epsilon} \frac{\delta \rho}{\rho}
\end{equation}
This is typically around 2 for metallic strain gauges.
In practice, strain measurements are typically of the order $10^{-3}$, so the change in resistance is small.
The resistivity and elastic modulus of the material are also temperature dependent which can cause errors in the measurement.
To measure these small changes in resistance a Wheatstone bridge is used.

\begin{equation}
    V_O = V_S \left( \frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2} - \frac{R_4}{R_3 + R_4} \right)
\end{equation}

Quarter-, half-, and full-bridge configurations of the Wheatstone bridge consist of different quantities and arrangements of strain gauges.
These give varying sensitivities and compensations for temperature effects and undesired components of forces.
The full bridge configuration is chosen for its high sensitivity and compensation \dots [?]

\section{Microphone}

The \textit{free-field response} of a microphone relates its output voltage with and the sound pressure that existed before the microphone was introduced into the sound field.
Microphones distort the pressure field because their acoustic impedance is very different from that of air.
Sound waves impinging on the body of the microphone give rise to complex reflections that depend on the frequency, the direction of propagation, and the shape of the microphone \cite{measurement_systems}
Wavelenghts of sound larger than the microphone, reflections are negligible at varying incidences.
For wavelengths much smaller however, the microphone is a significant obstacle and reflections are significant.
This means that the pressure measured by the microphone is twice what it would be if the microphone were not present \cite{measurement_systems}.

This means smaller microphones are required to measure high frequency sounds.


Three sets of sound pressure level readings over the frequency range of interest will be taken. \\
(1) Background level \\
(2) With only the motor in operation \\
(3) With the motor and propeller in operation \\

Measurements are to be taken at the beginning and end of each major test series.
